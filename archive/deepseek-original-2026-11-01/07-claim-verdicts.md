# 07 — Claim verdicts

Ten discrete claims, each with the strongest evidence for and against, a verdict, and a confidence level.

The confidence scale:

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Very high** | ≈95%+ — close to decisive |
| **High** | ≈80–95% — strong, with room for dissent |
| **Probable** | ≈60–80% — the balance of evidence, not certainty |
| **Contested** | ≈40–60% — genuinely unresolved |

---

## Claim 1 — Paul requires a head covering

**For:** 11:5–6, 13 make the covered head obligatory; 11:16 appeals to the practice of all the churches; the whole passage presupposes it.

**Against:** the hair-thesis reading (Payne, and the older Isaksson tradition) takes the "covering" to be hair. This is a minority but not negligible position; BDAG, Fee, Massey, Thiselton and most standard lexica reject it.

**Verdict: True. Confidence: very high.**

---

## Claim 2 — Paul requires a face covering

**For:** `κατακαλύπτω` can describe a full-face covering (BDAG's Theban parallel); some early Christians read it that way (Clement, Didascalia); if the point is modesty, a head-only covering is functionally thin; the "angels" may evoke Isa 6:2.

**Against:** no mention of the face anywhere in the passage; the object is `τὴν κεφαλήν`; the argument is about hair; the earliest Greek commentators (Chrysostom, Theodoret) say head only; no Corinthian evidence; no early Greek manuscript reads `κάλυμμα` at 11:10.

**Verdict: False. Confidence: very high.**

**Note:** "Paul does not require it" is compatible with "some Christians required it." The claim tested here is about Paul.

---

## Claim 3 — Paul forbids a face covering

**For:** nothing in the text forbids it. The nearest thing to a prohibition is the man's uncovered head (11:4), which concerns men, not women.

**Against:** the claim is sometimes inferred from "the covering is the head, not the face" — but a description of what is required is not a prohibition of more.

**Verdict: False. Confidence: high.**

---

## Claim 4 — Paul's covering normally left the face open

**For:** Roman matron's `palla` (Horace: "one can see only her face"); male `capite velato` in the iconography; Corinthian women's portraits mostly bare-headed, and the veiled ones are priestesses with faces visible; the hair/shaving logic; the earliest Christian interpreters; Stafford's late-antique corpus with no face-covering style; Tertullian's own definition of the veil by the hairline.

**Against:** the Oxford Bible Commentary says the himation "could be drawn ... over the face as well" and that married women "frequently covered their heads and even their faces"; Keener and Héring agree; Goulder argues the veil was drawn over the face for both sexes; the Jewish evidence (Jeremias) may include face-covering; Greek veiling gestures regularly involve the face.

**Verdict: Probable. Confidence: moderate–high.**

**Why not "high":** the opposing scholarly case is mainstream, not fringe, and the cultural evidence is genuinely mixed by region.

---

## Claim 5 — Face covering was practised by some early Christians

**For:** Clement of Alexandria (c. 190–200) requires it; the Didascalia presumes it (c. 230); Jerome and Ambrose praise it (late 4th c.); Tertullian admires it; Psellos' mother in the 11th c.; the `tapada` and `fazzuolo` in the 16th.

**Against:** no evidence before c. 190; Cyprian, Chrysostom and Theodoret do not mention it; Byzantine references are "quite rare" (Radle).

**Verdict: True. Confidence: high.**

---

## Claim 6 — Face covering was the universal early Christian practice

**For:** the *Didascalia* and Clement are church-order or catechetical texts, which suggests something more than private devotion.

**Against:** Chrysostom, the greatest Greek commentator, says head only; Cyprian, in Tertullian's own city a generation later, does not mention veils; the visual corpus shows no face-covering style; the practice is regionally clustered (Alexandria, Syria, later Rome/Milan ascetics).

**Verdict: False. Confidence: high.**

---

## Claim 7 — Face covering is a later Islamic or pagan import

**For:** face-covering is attested among pagan Arabs (Tertullian) and in Islamic societies; some Christian polemic treats it as foreign.

**Against:** Clement of Alexandria requires it two centuries before Islam, on Christian ascetic grounds; the Didascalia presumes it; Jerome and Ambrose praise it. Its Christian attestation is independent of Islam.

**Verdict: False as stated. Confidence: high.**

**Qualification:** some of its *cultural forms* (the `tapada`, the `burqa`-style full cover) are later and culturally specific. But the practice itself has Christian roots.

---

## Claim 8 — "Head covering" and "face covering" are the same thing

**For:** in some ancient dress, one garment covered both; the words overlap; a veil can cover head and face.

**Against:** the sources themselves distinguish them: Tertullian contrasts Christian head-covering with Arab head-**and**-face covering; the Didascalia speaks of covering the head with the robe and separately of concealing the face; BDAG distinguishes `κάλυμμα` (either) from `περίβολαιον` (body-wrap); Héring distinguishes the long face-veil from the short head-veil in the iconography.

**Verdict: False. Confidence: high.**

---

## Claim 9 — The face-veil reading of 1 Cor 11 arose c. 190–220 CE

**For:** the `κάλυμμα` variant appears in Irenaeus (Latin) and "Hippolytus"; Clement is the first to require the face; Nõmmik's reconstruction ties the two together.

**Against:** the reconstruction depends heavily on one scholar (Nõmmik); the variant's origin is unclear (he himself leaves it open, p. 302 n.22); it is possible that face-covering practice preceded the textual variant and caused it, rather than the reverse.

**Verdict: Well-supported, with the direction of causation uncertain. Confidence: high (for the date); contested (for the causal direction).**

---

## Claim 10 — The Greek word itself excludes the face

**For:** no lexicon defines `κατακαλύπτω` as a face-veil; Paul's object is the head.

**Against:** BDAG's Theban parallel shows the verb can describe a full-face covering; the word is generic.

**Verdict: False. Confidence: very high.**

## The two errors

### Error A — "the face-veil is the real meaning"

It claims that Paul's `κατακαλύπτω` means a face-veil, or that the `κάλυμμα` variant is original. Both are false. The variant has no Greek manuscript support; Paul never mentions the face; the argument of the passage runs on hair. This reading also has to explain why the covering is measured by the hairline in Tertullian and by the hair in Paul.

### Error B — "face-covering is not Christian"

It claims that face-covering is foreign to Christian practice. That is false. Clement, the Didascalia, Jerome, Ambrose and Tertullian (in different ways) attest it, and it has a continuous, if minority, history. It is an *extension* of the head covering, not a contradiction of it.

---

## The normative answer

**"Ought face coverings to be included in the head covering?"**

| Sense of "included" | Answer | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **In the definition** | **No** | Head covering = a covering on the head. Face covering is a distinct item that may accompany it. |
| **In the requirement** | **No** | Nothing in 1 Cor 11 requires it; the attempt to make it binding is exegetically unsupported. |
| **In the permitted practice** | **Yes** | Historically attested from the late 2nd c., defended by major writers, not contrary to Scripture. |
| **As the default** | **No** | The text's logic, the culture's default, the majority patristic reading, and the art all point to a covered head with an open face. |

**The one thing the evidence will not support is the claim that either position is a matter of obedience.**

## Appendix — the article that prompted the investigation

*"Why Christian Head Covering Is Not Face Veiling"* (Head Covering Movement; David Phillips). Its seven arguments and how they fared:

| Article's argument | Assessment |
|---|---|
| 1. `κατὰ κεφαλῆς ἔχων` (11:4) is vague and does not mention the face | **Correct.** Also does not exclude it. The Plutarch/Scipio comparison is fair and shows a head-covering *can* cover the face. |
| 2. `κατακαλύπτω` is generic and does not specify the object | **Correct — and self-defeating.** A generic word cannot exclude the face. |
| 3. The Shepherd of Hermas describes a head-covering, not a face-veil | **Broadly right**, but the argument is over-pressed: it is a fictional allegory describing one woman's veil. |
| 4. 11:10 says "on/upon her head" | **Correct.** A good locative argument, though it locates the item rather than defining its extent. |
| 5. `περίβολαιον` (11:15) is a different, wrap-around word | **Correct**, and a real argument: Paul uses the "wrapped around" word for hair, not for the cloth covering. |
| 6. The uncovered/shorn logic (11:5–6) requires a top-of-head covering | **Correct.** Probably the article's best internal argument. |
| 7. 1 Tim 2:9 "shamefacedness" is a KJV mistranslation | **Correct** as lexicography; it has little bearing on 1 Cor 11. |

**Overall:** the article is right on the exegesis of *requirement* and wrong on *exclusion*. Its strongest move is the argument from silence; its weakest is treating a generic verb as though it settled the question.
