## Evidence log — did "covering the head" also cover the FACE?

**Short answer from this corpus:** the normal head-covering (palla, amictus, himation, ricinium, suffibulum, mitra, kredemnon) left the face visible. Face-covering appears as a *distinct, marked gesture* — mourning, shame, bridal, or unusually strict "eastern"/Arabian/Jewish custom — not as the ordinary sense of "covered head." But the corpus is thinner than it looks: almost nothing first-century and Corinthian-specific settles it.

### Smith, E. (2014), *Female Head Covering in the Early Imperial Period* (MA thesis, Macquarie)

- [EVIDENCE] Horace, *Sat.* 1.2.94–100 (quoted p. 54): "In a matron one can see only her face, for unless she be a Catia, her long robe conceals all else." Body concealed, face exposed.
- [EVIDENCE] Lucan, *Pharsalia* 2.352–361 (quoted p. 66): "no flammeum, intended lightly to screen the bride's shy blushes, hid the downcast face." Bridal veil implied to cover head AND face.
- [EVIDENCE] Seneca the Elder, *Contr.* 2.7.6 (quoted p. 86): "did you not cover that face which could give the beholder such pleasure?" Face-covering is a discrete act a wife *should* have performed — not the default of head-covering.
- [EVIDENCE] Ovid, *Fasti* 2.817–820: Lucretia "for shame hid her face in her robe" (*amictu… ora*); Smith: "whether its meaning here is intended to depict covering of the head or the face, or both, remains somewhat ambiguous" (p. 39 n.20).
- [INTERPRETATION] Lexical chaos: "veil" "can often refer to anything from covering of the head, to the face, or both without differentiation" (p. 19). Much scholarly disagreement is terminological.
- [INTERPRETATION] Reports D'Ambra: in Roman art "the gesture of drawing the veil (to cover the face) usually expresses modesty or chastity," and Llewellyn-Jones' denial of any Greek/Roman distinction (p. 14 n.35). Smith herself is sceptical of Llewellyn-Jones' "veil-gesture" universalism.

### Stafford, G. (2024), *Veiling and Head-Covering in Late Antiquity* (Past & Present 263)

- [EVIDENCE] Three covering styles in a large visual corpus; face-covering is not among them: "Tight-fitting covers either concealed a woman's hair completely or left a portion visible around the face" (p. 14).
- [EVIDENCE] Loose veils "pinned near the top or the back of the head, revealing the hair" (p. 14); hairstyling under the cover is a major theme.
- [EVIDENCE] Egyptian textiles (Linscheid): >three-quarters close-fitting sprang caps; "All the female graves in Linscheid's study included a head-covering of some kind" (pp. 15–16).
- [EVIDENCE] "there is no discernible difference in veiling practices between the east and the west" (p. 18) — cuts against an eastern-face-veil/western-head-veil model.
- [EVIDENCE] Chiragan portrait heads: one cover "only covers the back of her head, leaving her face framed by thick locks of hair" (p. 37).
- [INTERPRETATION] Church Fathers "were swimming against the tide"; veiling was "highly flexible," filtered through status, fashion and practicality (p. 46).

### Harris, Brøns, Żuchowska (eds) (2022), *Textiles in Ancient Mediterranean Iconography*

- [EVIDENCE] Palmyrene female dress = tunic, himation, "and a veil covering the body from head to feet" (Żuchowska, p. 163).
- [EVIDENCE] "A veil, by definition, is a headdress rather than a mantle" — yet on reliefs it is often "impossible to decide … whether the garment … is a large mantle or a veil" (p. 165).
- [EVIDENCE] Veils "rectangular and … large enough to cover the whole body from head to feet" (p. 165); but the portraits are busts, so the face is displayed.
- [EVIDENCE] Glossary: ricinium "A square mantle, worn thrown back or double"; rica "A kerchief worn as a veil"; ricula "a veil thrown over the head by women during sacrifice" (pp. 200–201).
- [INTERPRETATION] Iconography is an "iconographic code," not a snapshot of daily dress (p. 165).

### Edmondson & Keith (eds) (2015), *Dressing Judeans and Christians in Antiquity*

- [EVIDENCE] Mishnah *Shabbat* 6:5–6: "Arab women [namely, Judean women of Arabia] may go out veiled [in an Arabian fashion]" (p. 26). "Arabian fashion" is a named regional usage — implying it differed from the ambient norm.
- [EVIDENCE] Same passage permits "a headdress hanging to the cheeks" (p. 26) — cheek-bands, not a face veil.
- [EVIDENCE] *Joseph and Aseneth* 18.5–6: "And with a veil she covered her head like a bride" (p. 142); her face is then described in detail (18.7–9). Covering = head.
- [INTERPRETATION] The Mishnah's categories concern Sabbath carrying, not modesty semantics (pp. 26–27).

### Marlowe, M. (2005), *Headcovering Customs of the Ancient World*

- [EVIDENCE] Greek art: "the headcovering is usually just the himation pulled over the top and back of the head"; sometimes "over the head and wrapped around the neck, without covering the face" (p. 3).
- [EVIDENCE] One bronze statuette (Metropolitan Museum) wraps the himation "across the lower part of her face … but this is unusual" — an Alexandrian dancer (p. 3). The corpus's clearest Greek face-veil image, flagged as exceptional.
- [EVIDENCE] flammeum "draped over the bride's head without covering her face" (p. 6); suffibulum "covered only the head (and perhaps the shoulders)"; ricinium "covered only the head and shoulders" (p. 5).
- [EVIDENCE] Jewish practice (citing Jeremias): a Jerusalem woman's "face was hidden by an arrangement of two head veils" (p. 8).
- [EVIDENCE] Dio Chrysostom on Tarsus: women so arrayed "that nobody could see any part of them, neither of the face nor of the rest of the body" (p. 17 n.12) — an extreme, local, civic custom.
- [INTERPRETATION] Polemic: "the failure of many commentators to make any distinction between face-veiling and headcovering" (p. 14 n.3) — names Oepke, Käsemann, Conzelmann, Thompson, Fee.

### Oster, R. E. (1988), "When Men Wore Veils to Worship" — limited

- [EVIDENCE] The supplied file is the publisher's abstract only; full text is paywalled. It defines its scope negatively: "the question of ancient Greek, Roman, and Jewish customs concerning the veiling of women … will not be broached here" (p. 481).
- [EVIDENCE] Oster's subject is male *capite velato* at sacrifice. He supplies no direct evidence on women's faces; his thesis reaches us via Gill and Massey.

### Gill, D. W. J. (1990), *TynBul* 41

- [EVIDENCE] Corinth: over-life-size Augustus from the Julian Basilica, toga "drawn up over his head," right hand missing but presumably holding a patera (p. 246); a Nero from Corinth likewise "partially covered by the toga" (p. 247).
- [EVIDENCE] Corinthian public marble portraits of women "are most frequently shown bare-headed" (p. 251).
- [EVIDENCE] Eumachia (Pompeii) "shows a woman wearing the stola over her head," holding a patera (p. 252) — female *capite velato*, face visible.
- [EVIDENCE] Ara Pacis: the veiled women "appear to be the Vestal Virgins"; "most other women in the frieze do not seem to have their heads covered" (p. 252).
- [EVIDENCE] Tiberius coins: a woman (Pietas or Livia) with a stephane "over which is draped a veil" (p. 252).
- [INTERPRETATION] *capite velato* is "the iconographical mark of a sacrificant presiding over a specifically Roman ritual" (Gordon, quoted p. 247), "confined to the priests" and active participants (p. 248) — a role signal, not face-concealment.

### Massey, P. T. (2015), *JGRChJ* 11

- [EVIDENCE] Andania (95–90 BCE): "The men are given no instructions not to wear a veil; the women are given no instructions for wearing one" (p. 59).
- [EVIDENCE] Pausanias 10.25.10: Andromache and Medesicaste are "wearing their veils" while Polyxena's hair is braided "according to the custom of virgins" (p. 60 n.31).
- [EVIDENCE] Homer, *Il.* 22.468–470: the κρήδεμνον is removed last, after the hair-bindings; "A woman may remove her veil without unbinding her hair," and may wear hair "either up or down … and topped off with a veil" (p. 61 n.32).
- [INTERPRETATION] Against Delling (1931), Jaubert (1972), Økland (2004): "the wearing of a veil by married women is generally held by most classical scholars to be the Greek norm" (p. 54).
- [INTERPRETATION] He concedes silence: "This is all that can be extracted from the Andania inscription regarding veiling" (p. 59).

### Massey, P. T. (2018), *JBL* 137

- [EVIDENCE] κατακαλύπτω and κατὰ κεφαλῆς ἔχων "describe the covering of the head with a material veil," never loose hair (p. 501).
- [EVIDENCE] Livy yields 14 head-covering references, and "all of these texts describe only men" (p. 505).
- [EVIDENCE] Josephus, *J.W.* 7.128: Vespasian "veiled most of his head" before praying (p. 504 n.14).
- [INTERPRETATION] Footnote: "Michael D. Goulder argues just the opposite: the veil or hood is drawn down over the face for both males and females" (p. 504 n.12).

## Where sources disagree

- **The flammeum and the face:** Marlowe says it was worn "without covering her face" (p. 6); Smith, from Lucan, infers it "covered both the head and the face of the bride" (p. 66).
- **Whether Greek women were routinely veiled at all:** Marlowe finds "no good evidence" for compulsion and calls the hetaera/bare-head claim baseless (pp. 3–4); Massey (2015, p. 54) and the Galt–Llewellyn-Jones line treat routine veiling as the Greek norm. Massey explicitly names Delling, Jaubert, Økland, Hays and Murphy-O'Connor as opponents.
- **Face or head under the covering:** Goulder (via Massey 2018, p. 504 n.12) says face for both sexes; Gill, Marlowe, Edmondson and Stafford's image corpus assume not.
- **Greek vs Roman gesture:** D'Ambra separates Attic "lifting the veil" (sexual availability) from Roman "drawing the veil" (modesty); Llewellyn-Jones denies the distinction (Smith p. 14 n.35).
- **East vs West:** Marlowe, Jeremias and Oepke posit a strong eastern face-veiling norm; Stafford (p. 18) finds no east/west divergence, and Edmondson's Mishnah makes "Arabian" veiling a *special case* rather than the baseline.
- **Ancient sources contradict each other:** Valerius Maximus 6.3.10 says Sulpicius Gallus divorced his wife for going *uncovered*; Plutarch (*RQ* 14) says the opposite (Smith pp. 13–14).

## What remains unknown

- No first-century Corinthian text, statue or coin specifies whether a woman's covering at worship reached the face. Gill's female Corinthian portraits are mostly bare-headed, and the *capite velato* statues are male, priestly and face-visible.
- Whether the Andania/Lycosura regulations presuppose veils or bare heads is undecidable from the inscriptions themselves (Massey 2015, p. 59).
- Whether Greek κρήδεμνον/κάλυμμα could be drawn across the face in ordinary (non-mourning, non-ritual) use; the one Greek image Marlowe cites is a single anomalous statuette.
- Oster's full argument could not be checked — only the paywalled abstract was available, so claims about his treatment of women's veils should be sourced via Gill/Massey, not Oster directly.
