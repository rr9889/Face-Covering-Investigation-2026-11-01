# 03 — Greco-Roman, Corinthian and Jewish background

**Question for this file:** in the world of Paul's churches, when a woman "covered her head," did the covering normally reach the face? Was face-covering normal, optional, rare, or a distinct gesture?

**Short answer:** the ordinary covering left the face visible; face-covering was a marked gesture (mourning, shame, bridal, extreme modesty) or a regional custom ("Arabian," Tarsian, some Jewish practice). But the evidence is not unanimous, and the best mainstream commentary (Oxford) explicitly allows that married women "frequently covered their heads and even their faces."

---

## 1. Roman women

### 1.1 The ordinary covering

The Roman matron's outer garment — `palla`, `amictus`, sometimes `ricinium` — was drawn over the head. Its effect is described by Horace:

> "In a matron one can see only her face, for unless she be a Catia, her long robe conceals all else." — Horace, *Sat.* 1.2.94–100, quoted in Smith 2014: 54

Body concealed, **face visible**. This is the single clearest literary statement of the default.

### 1.2 Face-covering as a distinct act

Face-covering appears as something a woman *does*, not as the ordinary state of being covered:

- **Seneca the Elder**, *Controversiae* 2.7.6: a husband reproaches his wife — "did you not cover that face which could give the beholder such pleasure?" (Smith 2014: 86). The face-covering is a discrete act of modesty she failed to perform.
- **Ovid**, *Fasti* 2.817–820: Lucretia "for shame hid her face in her robe." Smith notes the ambiguity: "whether its meaning here is intended to depict covering of the head or the face, or both, remains somewhat ambiguous" (2014: 39 n.20).
- **Valerius Maximus** 6.3.10: Sulpicius Gallus divorced his wife because she had gone out `capite aperto` — with head uncovered (Smith 2014: 13–14).

### 1.3 A genuine ancient contradiction

Valerius Maximus treats the veiling of a wife as obligatory; Plutarch (*Quaest. Rom.* 14) describes Roman worship with the head covered but does not make the same claim about women's daily dress. Smith uses the pair to argue that the custom was contested, not uniform (2014: 13–14).

### 1.4 The bridal veil (`flammeum`) — disputed

- **Smith** (2014: 66), from Lucan, *Pharsalia* 2.352–361: "no flammeum, intended lightly to screen the bride's shy blushes, hid the downcast face" — therefore the bridal veil covered head **and** face.
- **Marlowe** (2005: 6): the flammeum was "draped over the bride's head without covering her face."

Both are reading the same Latin poetry differently. This is a good example of how unstable the "veil = face" assumption is even for a garment designed for modesty.

## 2. Roman men — `capite velato`

This is the background for 1 Cor 11:4, and it matters because it shows what a *head* covering looked like in Paul's world.

- Roman priests and officiants drew the toga over the head at sacrifice and prayer. The gesture is `capite velato`, "with covered head."
- In the iconography, **the face is visible**. Nõmmik: "In ancient depictions of this ritual gesture, the facial features are clearly visible, and the garment seems to be neatly arranged so that part of the head and hair remain visible as well" (2025, ch. 2).
- Examples: Augustus from the Via Labicana; the Ara Pacis procession (Augustus, Agrippa, Livia, all head-covered, faces visible); the Pompeii altar; the Corinthian portraits (Gill 1990: 246–252); Josephus, *J.W.* 7.128 (Vespasian "veiled most of his head" before praying).
- Greek description: Dionysius of Halicarnassus 15.9.2 — `τὴν περιβολὴν κατὰ κεφαλῆς εἵλκυσε`, "he drew his cloak down over his head" (cited at Massey 2018: n.13).

**Oster's thesis** (1988) is that this Roman gesture is the matrix for 1 Cor 11:4. His article is represented in the corpus only by its abstract, which explicitly excludes women's veiling from its scope: "the question of ancient Greek, Roman, and Jewish customs concerning the veiling of women … will not be broached here" (Oster 1988: 481).

**Implication for the face question:** the male counterpart to the woman's covering is a head-covering with an open face. If Paul's rule for women mirrors the same garment-logic, the natural expectation is a covered head, not a covered face.

**But note:** Plutarch describes Scipio the Younger covering **both head and face** with his toga (`kata tēs kephalēs echōn to himation`), and this is the passage the popular article used. It shows that a garment over the head *can* continue over the face. It does not show that `capite velato` normally did.

## 3. Greek women

### 3.1 Vocabulary

`κρήδεμνον` (Homeric head-veil), `καλύπτρα`, `κάλυμμα`, `θέριστρον`, `μίτρα`. The terms overlap and none is a technical term for "face-veil."

### 3.2 The veil as gesture

In Homer and tragedy the veil is held across the face as a gesture of `αἰδώς`:

- **Penelope** holds her shining veil across her face before the suitors (Wu 2020: 41, 65).
- **Phaedra** (Euripides, *Hippolytus* 243–245): "Nurse, cover my head up again. For I am ashamed of my words" (Wu 2020: 67).
- **Heracles**: "I will wrap darkness around my head, for I am ashamed" (Wu 2020: 67).

So face-covering is a well-attested *gesture* in Greek culture — for shame, grief, and concealment.

### 3.3 The ordinary head-covering in art

Marlowe: "the headcovering is usually just the himation pulled over the top and back of the head"; sometimes "over the head and wrapped around the neck, without covering the face" (2005: 3). He notes one bronze statuette (Metropolitan Museum) in which the himation is wrapped "across the lower part of her face … but this is unusual."

### 3.4 The scholarly split

- **Llewellyn-Jones** (*Aphrodite's Tortoise*) treats the veil as "any garment that covers the head or the face," and argues for a pervasive Greek veiling culture.
- **Smith** (2014: 19–21) criticises exactly this: the term "can often refer to anything from covering of the head, to the face, or both without differentiation," which makes the thesis less surprising than its title. Smith refuses the word "veil" altogether and specifies which body part is covered.
- **Massey** (2015: 54): "the wearing of a veil by married women is generally held by most classical scholars to be the Greek norm."

The disagreement is partly semantic. It is not settled.

### 3.5 Tarsus — an extreme local custom

Dio Chrysostom, *Or.* 33.48 (Tarsian Discourse), on the women of Tarsus:

> "they should dress and go about in such a manner that no one may see any part of them, neither of the face nor of the rest of the body" (Chadwick 2022: 7–8)

This is regularly cited as proof that ancient women covered their faces. Nõmmik responds that "there is no reason to believe that all women of first-century [Tarsus] dressed this way" (2025: 40), and Chadwick notes that in the same context young unmarried girls were *not* expected to cover head or face.

## 4. Corinth specifically

The Corinthian evidence is thin but consistent:

| Data | Source | Point |
|---|---|---|
| Public marble portraits of elite Corinthian women "are most frequently shown bare-headed" | Gill 1990: 251 | Covering was not universal |
| A veiled Corinthian woman appears as a priestess in `capite velato` (Eumachia type), face visible | Gill 1990: 252 | The veiled woman in local iconography is a ritual role, face open |
| Augustus and Nero portraits from Corinth, toga over the head | Gill 1990: 246–247 | Male `capite velato` in the colony |
| No first-century Corinthian text, coin, or statue specifies a face-veil | — | Absence of evidence |

**Assessment:** Corinth gives us a *male* head-covering with the face open, and *female* portraits that are mostly bare-headed. It gives us no face-veils and no evidence that Christian women there wore them. This does not prove they did not; it means the face-veil reading has no Corinthian anchor.

## 5. Jewish practice

This is the most contested item in the background material, and it matters because some scholars derive Paul's rule from a Jewish custom.

### 5.1 The evidence

- **Mishnah *Ketubbot* 7:6** lists things that transgress "the custom of Jewish women" — going out with the head uncovered is one. Barrett cites it (1962: 249) via Schlatter to argue the reference is to "the practice of the devout and modest Jewess."
- **Mishnah *Shabbat* 6:6**: "Arabian women may go out veiled" — i.e. in an Arabian fashion, named as a regional usage distinct from the ambient norm (Edmondson & Keith 2015: 26).
- **Jeremias** (as reported by Marlowe 2005: 8): a Jerusalem woman's face "was hidden by an arrangement of two head veils, a head-band on the forehead with bands to the chin, and a hairnet with ribbons and knots, so that her features could not be recognized."

### 5.2 The dispute

- If Jeremias is right, first-century Jewish women *did* cover the lower face in public, and a Jewish-background reading of 1 Cor 11 would include the face.
- Marlowe doubts it is Paul's background: "We would expect him to use a word or expression for the face-covering or veil (`κάλυμμα`), at least, but that is not the case here" (2005: 10).
- Nõmmik notes that the rabbinic sources are late relative to Paul (2025: 35 n.13).
- Barrett, who knows the Jewish material best among the commentators, concludes the covering conceals "the hair and upper part of the body, **not** … the face" (1962: 249).

**Assessment:** the Jewish datum is real but late and contested as a background. It cannot carry the weight some older scholarship put on it.

## 6. "Eastern" and Arabian practice

Face-covering is repeatedly located to the east:

| Source | Statement |
|---|---|
| Tertullian, *Virg.* 17 | "Arabia's heathen females … cover not only the head, but the face also … with one eye free" |
| Dio Chrysostom 33.48 | Tarsian women cover the face |
| Mishnah *Shabbat* 6:6 | "Arabian" veiling is a named regional fashion |
| Keener 2005: 92 | "Far to the east and in Arabia, modest wives might wear even face veils" |
| MacMullen (in Smith 2014: 19 n.52) | "Veiling meant the head, only in Arabia the face as well" |

**Assessment:** the east is where face-covering is attested; the Greco-Roman default is the head. Tertullian's use of the Arab women is rhetorical — he is shaming Carthaginian Christians by comparison, not describing their practice.

## 7. Art and iconography

### 7.1 The late-antique visual corpus

Stafford 2024 is the largest survey in the corpus. Her findings:

- Three covering styles: tight-fitting caps, loose veils draped over the head, and veils pinned at the back of the head.
- "Tight-fitting covers either concealed a woman's hair completely or left a portion visible around the face" (2024: 14).
- "In terms of regional distinctions, there is no discernible difference in veiling practices between the east and the west" (2024: 18).
- Egyptian textile evidence: more than three-quarters of the excavated caps are close-fitting sprang caps; "All the female graves in Linscheid's study included a head-covering of some kind" (2024: 15–16).

**No face-covering style appears in her corpus.** That is a significant negative result.

### 7.2 Christian art

- Catacomb figures wear head-only caps (Marlowe 2005: 5).
- The Dura-Europos synagogue murals are sometimes cited for Jewish women's dress, but Marlowe warns it is "very precarious" to use them as evidence of normal attire (2005: n.15).
- The popular claim that "early Christian art shows unveiled faces" is directionally right but imprecise: the art shows **veiled heads with visible faces**, not bare heads.

### 7.3 The limits of iconography

Harris, Brøns and Żuchowska (2022: 165) make the methodological point: iconography is an "iconographic code," not a snapshot of daily dress; and on reliefs it is often "impossible to decide … whether the garment … is a large mantle or a veil." Art can show that faces were visible; it cannot show that they were *meant* to be.

---

## 8. Verdict on the background

| Proposition | Verdict | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| The ordinary Roman woman's head-covering left the face visible | True | high |
| Male `capite velato` left the face visible | True | very high |
| Face-covering was a marked gesture, not the default | True | high |
| Face-covering was normal in some eastern regions | True | high |
| Corinthian Christian women wore face-veils | No evidence | — (unknown) |
| Jewish women covered the face in Paul's time | Disputed | contested |
| The Greek East routinely covered faces | Disputed | contested |

**Bottom line:** the background evidence makes a *covered head with an open face* the natural reading of "covered head" in Paul's world. It does not make a face-covering impossible — and in some regions it was real.
