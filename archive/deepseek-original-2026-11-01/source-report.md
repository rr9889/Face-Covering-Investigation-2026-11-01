# Does the head covering include the face?

**An evidence review of 1 Corinthians 11:2–16, the Greco-Roman background, and early Christian practice**

Prepared from the corpus in `C:\Users\Admin\Documents\Early Christianity\Head Coverings` (≈200 text sources, 112 PDFs, primary and secondary), with external checks of primary texts. I began with no position; the verdicts below are where the evidence actually landed.

---

## 0. How to read the verdicts

I separate three questions that are usually run together:

1. **Exegetical** — did Paul require, permit, or say nothing about covering the face?
2. **Historical** — did Christians in fact cover the face as part of this practice?
3. **Normative** — ought face covering to be included in "the head covering" today?

Confidence labels: **very high** (≈95%+), **high** (≈80–95%), **probable** (≈60–80%), **contested** (≈40–60%).

## 1. The text

### 1.1 The words

| Term | Reference | Lexical sense (BDAG) | Object stated? |
|---|---|---|---|
| `κατακαλύπτω` | 11:6, 7 | "cover, veil" | at 11:7, `τὴν κεφαλήν` |
| `ἀκατακάλυπτος` | 11:5, 13 | "uncovered" | dative `τῇ κεφαλῇ` |
| `κατὰ κεφαλῆς ἔχων` | 11:4 | "having [something] down over the head" | object unexpressed |
| `περίβολαιον` | 11:15 | "wrap, cloak, robe" (covers much of the body) | of hair |
| `πρόσωπον` | — | "face" | **never occurs in 11:2–16** |

Two facts are not in dispute:

- Every relevant term is defined by reference to the **head**, not the face. No lexicon defines any of them as a face-veil.
- Paul's only expressed object is the head. He never says "face" in the passage.

### 1.2 What the argument actually turns on

Paul's reasoning runs on hair and head: uncovered = shaved (vv. 5–6); long hair is glory/dishonour (vv. 14–15); the covering sits "on/upon the head" (`ἐπὶ τῆς κεφαλῆς`, v. 10); the man's case is "something down over the head" (v. 4). The logic is scalp-centred. A shaved head and a covered head are the two poles of the argument, and neither pole involves the face. If the covering were essentially a face-veil, vv. 5–6 would be arguing about a body part that has no hair.

### 1.3 The strongest argument for "head only"

Paul uses the face-veil word elsewhere. In 2 Cor 3:13–16 he twice uses `κάλυμμα` for the veil **on Moses' face**, and in 3:18 he speaks of "unveiled **faces**" (`ἀνακεκαλυμμένῳ προσώπῳ`). He had the vocabulary and used it when he meant it. In 1 Cor 11 he does not.

That is the single most weighty datum in the whole debate. It is an argument from silence, but a *loaded* silence: the same author, in the same letter collection, with the same conceptual world, describes a face-veil explicitly — and does not do so here.

**Verdict 1: Paul commands a covering on the head and says nothing about the face. Confidence: very high.**

### 1.4 The strongest counter-argument

`κατακαλύπτω` is generic: "cover, veil." It does not specify the object, so it does not exclude the face. BDAG itself, at 11:7, cites Ps.-Dicaearchus on Theban women, whose head-covering was of such a kind that "the whole face seems to be covered as with a mask" (BDAG 458). So a head-covering *can* cover the whole face, and the verb can describe that. The same lexicon notes that the Shepherd of Hermas describes a woman "veiled to the forehead" (`κατακεκαλυμμένη ἕως τοῦ μετώπου`), which marks a lower limit at the forehead — i.e. the face below it is open, but the veil *reaches* the forehead.

So the lexical evidence is genuinely two-sided:

- it does not *require* the face;
- it does not *exclude* the face;
- the ordinary instantiation of "covering the head" in the surrounding culture usually left the face visible;
- but a covering that drapes over the face still satisfies the word.

**Verdict 2: The text permits face covering; it does not require it. Confidence: high.**

### 1.5 Two internal arguments for the face reading, assessed

**(a) "Head" can mean the whole person.** `κεφαλή` is sometimes used for the person (BDAG notes Murphy-O'Connor's "his head" = "himself" at 11:4). If "cover your head" means "cover yourself," the face is not excluded. But in this passage the head is unmistakably the physical head — it grows hair, it is shaved, it is anointed, it wears the covering "upon" it. The synecdoche is present in the *theological* register ("dishonours his head"), not in the *dress* register.

**(b) "Because of the angels" (v. 10).** If the angels are the seraphim of Isaiah 6:2, who cover their faces before God, then the woman is being told to imitate a face-covering. Robertson and Plummer suggested this; Fitzmyer doubts it, and the more common readings are the Watchers of Gen 6/1 Enoch (who lusted after women) or guardian angels who oversee worship. The verse says "because of the angels," not "like the angels," so the inference is analogical, not textual. It gives the face reading a foothold, not a foundation.

**(c) The force of `κατά`.** Marlowe argues that `κατα-` means "thoroughly covered"; Nõmmik and Wu take it as "down" (a garment coming *down* from/over the head). If "down over" is right, the mental picture is a cloth descending over the head — which may continue over the face. But the prefix describes direction, not extent.

### 1.6 The textual variant at 11:10

Some witnesses read "a woman ought to have **a veil** (`κάλυμμα`) on her head" instead of "**authority**" (`ἐξουσίαν`). The evidence:

- There is no early Greek manuscript with `κάλυμμα` (Nõmmik 2025: 300).
- The reading appears in later Latin and Bohairic translations and in patristic citations — Irenaeus (*Haer.* 1.8.2, Latin), Hippolytus (*Comm. Dan.*), and later Latin writers (Nõmmik 2025: 300–301; Héring 1962: 108).
- BDAG lists it as a variant at 11:10.

This variant is important not because it is original — it is not — but because it shows what early readers *thought the verse meant*: a material veil. It does not settle face vs. head, because `κάλυμμα` is used both for a woman's headdress (Philo) and for Moses' face-veil (2 Cor 3:13). But it is the hinge on which the later face-covering reading turns.

**Verdict 3: The variant is secondary but historically revealing. Confidence: high.**

## 2. The surrounding culture

### 2.1 Roman

The ordinary Roman matron's covering was the `palla`/`amictus` drawn over the head. Horace describes the effect: "In a matron one can see only her face, for unless she be a Catia, her long robe conceals all else" (*Sat.* 1.2.94–100, quoted in Smith 2014: 54). The body is concealed; **the face is what remains visible**.

Male `capite velato` — the gesture Paul arguably prohibits in v. 4 — drapes the garment over the head and leaves the face open. This is visible across the iconography: Augustus, Agrippa, Livia, the Pompeii altar, the Corinthian portraits (Gill 1990: 246–252; Nõmmik 2025, ch. 2).

Face-covering in Roman contexts is a *discrete gesture*, not the default: mourning, shame, the bridal `flammeum` (disputed — see below), or the very strict wife of Seneca's *Controversiae* 2.7.6 who is blamed for *not* having covered "that face."

Two ancient sources even contradict each other on whether a Roman wife was obliged to cover at all: Valerius Maximus 6.3.10 (Sulpicius Gallus divorced his wife for going out `capite aperto`) versus Plutarch *Quaest. Rom.* 14 (Smith 2014: 13–14). The custom was real but not uniform.

### 2.2 Greek

Greek has a rich veil vocabulary — `κρήδεμνον`, `καλύπτρα`, `κάλυμμα`, `θέριστρον`. In Homer and tragedy the veil is held **across the face** as a gesture of `αἰδώς` (modesty/shame): Penelope holds her shining veil across her face before the suitors; Phaedra asks the nurse to cover her head again (Wu 2020: 41, 66–67). Mourning and shame regularly involve the face.

But the ordinary Greek head-covering in art is the himation pulled over the top and back of the head, face open; the one vase/statue example of a himation drawn across the lower face is flagged by Marlowe as "unusual" (Marlowe 2005: 3).

Dio Chrysostom's Tarsian discourse (33.48) describes women veiled so that "no one may see any part of them, neither of the face nor of the rest of the body" — but this is a *local civic custom*, cited by scholars as an extreme case, not the Greek norm (Chadwick 2022: 7–8; Nõmmik 2025: 40).

### 2.3 Corinth

The Corinth-specific evidence points the same way:

- Public marble portraits of elite Corinthian women are "most frequently shown bare-headed" (Gill 1990: 251). Covering was not universal even among the elite.
- Where women are veiled in Corinthian/Pompeian iconography, it is the `capite velato` of a priestess (Eumachia), face visible (Gill 1990: 252).
- No first-century Corinthian text, coin, or statue specifies a face-veil.

### 2.4 "Eastern," Arabian, Jewish

Face-covering is attested as a regional intensification:

- Tertullian: "Arabia's heathen females ... cover not only the head, but the face also, so entirely that they are content, with one eye free" (*Virg.* 17).
- Mishnah *Shabbat* 6:6: "Arabian women may go out veiled" — named as a *distinctive* regional fashion (Edmondson & Keith 2015: 26).
- Dio's Tarsus (above).
- **Jewish practice is contested.** Jeremias argued that a Jerusalem woman's face was hidden by two head-veils plus a head-band and chin-bands, "so that her features could not be recognized" (Marlowe 2005: 8, quoting Jeremias). Marlowe himself doubts this is Paul's background: "We would expect him to use a word or expression for the face-covering or veil (`κάλυμμα`), at least, but that is not the case here" (Marlowe 2005: 10). Nõmmik adds that the rabbinic sources are late (2025: 35 n.13). So the Jewish datum is real but late and disputed as a background.

**Verdict 4: In the ambient culture of Paul's churches, "covered head" normally meant a head covering with the face open; face-covering was a marked, regional, or situational practice. Confidence: high for Rome/Corinth; probable for the Greek East generally.**

Two mainstream commentaries push back: the Oxford Bible Commentary says the himation "could be drawn forward ... over the face as well," and that married women "frequently covered their heads and even their faces" (1125–1126); Keener says that "far to the east and in Arabia, modest wives might wear even face veils," while in Jerusalem, Tarsus and for some Corinthian women "it sufficed to cover the hair" (Keener 2005: 92). MacMullen's oft-cited summary is the other way: "Veiling meant the head, only in Arabia the face as well" (in Smith 2014: 19 n.52). The disagreement is real; the balance of the evidence still favours the face-open default for Corinth, but the face-covered option was not exotic.

## 3. The churches

### 3.1 The first century and a half

There is no explicit Christian evidence for or against face-covering before the late second century. The Apostolic Fathers are silent. The earliest interpreters who quote 11:10 (Irenaeus, Hippolytus) read "veil," but they are quoting a variant, not exegeting the face.

### 3.2 The shift, c. 190–220

Nõmmik's reception-history argument (2025: 300–301, 315–317) is the most important single contribution in the corpus:

1. Some early readers replaced `ἐξουσία` with `κάλυμμα` at 11:10 (no Greek MS support), making "veil" the subject of the verse.
2. Because `κάλυμμα` denotes an item whose purpose was head **and** face, attention shifted to the face.
3. Ascetic anxiety about women's visibility, plus reading through 1 Tim 2 and Eph 5, completed the shift.

"Beginning with the end of the second century CE, many interpreters of this passage focused their attention solely on the necessity for women to cover their heads and faces" (Nõmmik 2025: 301).

### 3.3 The Fathers who included the face

- **Clement of Alexandria** (*Paed.* 2.10; 3.11), c. 190–200: the head is to be veiled **and the face covered** — "it has also been enjoined that the head should be veiled and the face covered" (Leone 2012: 281 n.22); women are to wear a covering "that conceals their eyes," and the woman who does not bare her face but covers it with a veil "prevents anyone else from sinning" (Nõmmik 2025: 301–302). Note: Clement *paraphrases* 11:13 rather than quoting it, uses `ἐγκαλύπτω` (complete concealment) rather than Paul's `κατακαλύπτω`, and grounds the practice in female "weakness" — a rationale Paul does not give. This is the earliest Christian text that clearly requires the face.
- **Tertullian** (*Virg.* 17), c. 200–213: wants the veil to cover the whole head to the neck, and admires the Arab women who cover the face. But he also defines the veil's extent as co-extensive with the hair: "The region of the veil is co-extensive with the space covered by the hair when unbound" — i.e. head and neck, not face. And he is arguing *against* Christian women who were not covering as he wished. Radle concludes he "does not advocate facial veils for Christian women" (2019: 1085 n.71).
- **Didascalia Apostolorum** (N. Syria, c. 230, 1.8–9): "cover your head with your robe so that your great beauty is concealed," and it presumes that a woman conceals her face in public (Radle 2019: 1086); "if thou coverest thy face from strange men with a veil of modesty."
- **Jerome** (*Ep.* 130.18, 414) praises a woman who "veils all of her face except her eyes"; **Ambrose** (*De poenitentia* 1.14) covers "head (`caput`)" and "face (`vultus`)" (Radle 2019: 1086 n.77). Both are ascetic ideals, not universal law.
- Later: a minority stream, including an eleventh-century Byzantine case (Psellos' mother) who "veiled her entire face with modesty" (Radle 2019: 1091–1092).

### 3.4 The Fathers who did not

- **Chrysostom** (*Hom. 1 Cor.* 26): reads the covering as the sign of subjection; no face.
- **Theodoret**: "Paul calls the veil authority" (Kovacs 2005: 183); no face.
- **Byzantine practice**: veiling of hair and head, not face; references to covering the face exist but "are not as common as admonitions to cover the hair," and a face veil is a minority, later phenomenon (Radle 2019: 1216–1218).

### 3.5 Art

Stafford's late-antique visual corpus (2024: 14–18) finds three covering styles — tight-fitting caps, loose veils revealing hair, and veils pinned at the back — and **no face-covering style**. "There is no discernible difference in veiling practices between the east and the west" (p. 18). Catacomb figures wear head-only caps (Marlowe 2005: 5). The Dura-Europos synagogue murals are sometimes cited, but Marlowe himself warns it is "very precarious" to use them as evidence of normal attire (2005: n.15).

The frequently repeated claim that "early Christian art shows unveiled faces" is directionally right but loosely stated: the art shows *veiled heads with visible faces*, not bare heads.

### 3.6 Timeline

| Date | Place | Evidence | Face? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1st c. | Corinth | Paul, 1 Cor 11 | head only; `πρόσωπον` absent |
| c. 190–200 | Alexandria | Clement, *Paed.* 2.10; 3.11 | **head + face required** |
| c. 200–213 | Carthage | Tertullian, *Virg.* 17 | head/neck; Arab face-veiling admired, not imposed |
| c. 230 | N. Syria | Didascalia 1.8–9 | head; face presumed concealed in public |
| c. 250 | Carthage | Cyprian, *De habitu virginum* | hair, dress, cosmetics — no veil rule |
| c. 390–414 | Antioch/Rome | Chrysostom, *Hom.* 26; Jerome, *Ep.* 130.18; Ambrose, *De paen.* 1.14 | Chrysostom: head only; Jerome/Ambrose: face, as ascetic ideal |
| 11th–12th c. | Constantinople | Psellos; Radle 2019 | face veil rare, elite |
| 16th c. | Spain/Italy | *tapada*, *fazzuolo*, *cappa* | face covering becomes fashion |

The pattern is not a straight line from Paul to the face-veil, nor from Paul to the bare face. It is: **head covering everywhere; face covering as a regional and ascetic intensification, first clearly required in Alexandria around 190–200, never made universal.**

**Verdict 5: Face covering is a real Christian practice from the late 2nd–3rd century, regional and ascetic in character, taught by some major writers; it was never the universal or earliest Christian norm. Confidence: high.**

## 4. The claims, tested

| # | Claim | Verdict | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Paul requires a head covering | **True** | very high |
| 2 | Paul requires a face covering | **False** | very high |
| 3 | Paul forbids a face covering | **False** | high |
| 4 | Paul's covering normally left the face open | **Probable** | moderate–high |
| 5 | Face covering was practiced by some early Christians | **True** | high |
| 6 | Face covering was the universal early practice | **False** | high |
| 7 | Face covering is a later Islamic/pagan import | **False as stated** | high |
| 8 | "Head covering" and "face covering" are the same thing | **False** | high |
| 9 | The face-veil reading of 1 Cor 11 arose c. 190–220 CE | **Well-supported** | high |
| 10 | The Greek word itself excludes the face | **False** | very high |

### The two errors to avoid

**Error A (the "face-veil is the real meaning" error).** It claims Paul's `κατακαλύπτω` means a face-veil, or that the `κάλυμμα` variant is original. Both are false: the variant has no Greek manuscript support, and Paul never mentions the face. This reading also has to explain why Paul's argument is about hair.

**Error B (the "face-covering is not Christian" error).** It claims face covering is foreign to Christian practice. That is false: Clement, the Didascalia, and Tertullian (in different ways) commend it, and it has a continuous, if minority, history. It is an extension of the head covering, not a contradiction of it.

### The honest middle

The evidence supports a distinction the polemics usually collapse:

- **Head covering** is the dominical requirement, and it is about the head.
- **Face covering** is an optional intensification, historically Christian, culturally conditioned, and neither commanded nor forbidden.
- The two are **not the same practice**, but they are **compatible practices**, and a woman may combine them.

## 5. Answer to the question

**"Ought face coverings to be included in the head covering?"**

- **In the definition?** No. The definition should be "a covering on the head." Face covering is a distinct item that may accompany it.
- **In the requirement?** No. Nothing in 1 Cor 11 requires it; the attempt to make it binding is exegetically unsupported.
- **In the permitted practice?** Yes. It is historically attested, theologically defensible, and not contrary to Scripture.
- **In the recommended practice?** Prudentially, no, as a default. The text's logic, the cultural default of the first churches, the majority patristic reading, and the art all point to a covered head with an open face. Face covering is best treated as a contextual or ascetic option, not the standard.

The one thing the evidence will not support is the claim that either position is a matter of obedience.

## 6. What would change my mind

- A first-century Greek text using `κατακαλύπτω` with a woman's face as its object, or a Corinthian image of a Christian woman with a face-veil.
- Evidence that `κάλυμμα` at 11:10 is original (it is not, on current manuscript evidence).
- Evidence that Paul's "because of the angels" is modelled on Isaiah 6:2 (angels covering faces) rather than on the Watchers tradition or on liturgical order — this would give the face reading an internal foothold it currently lacks.
