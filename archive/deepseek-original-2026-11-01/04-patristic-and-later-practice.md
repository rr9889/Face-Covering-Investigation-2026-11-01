# 04 — Patristic and later Christian practice

**Question for this file:** what did Christian writers and churches actually do about the face, and when?

**Short answer:** the first Christian text that clearly requires face-covering is Clement of Alexandria, c. 190–200. Before that, silence. After that, a mixed record: some writers require or praise it (Clement, the *Didascalia*, Jerome, Ambrose), some describe a head-covering that stops at the hairline (Tertullian's own rule, Chrysostom, Theodoret), and most of the later church veils the hair and head, not the face. Face-covering is a real Christian practice, but a regional and ascetic one, never a universal norm.

---

## 1. The silence of the first 150 years

No Christian text before the late second century discusses the face in connection with 1 Cor 11.

- The Apostolic Fathers do not comment on the passage.
- The earliest quotations of 11:10 (Irenaeus, "Hippolytus") quote the **veil** variant, not the face.
- The earliest Christian art (catacombs, 3rd–4th c.) shows veiled heads with visible faces.

This silence is not proof that no Christian woman covered her face; it means we have no evidence that anyone required or discussed it.

---

## 2. The turn: the `κάλυμμα` reading of 11:10

The mechanism by which the face entered the interpretation is documented in Nõmmik 2025: 300–301.

1. Some second-century readers replaced `ἐξουσία` ("authority") with `κάλυμμα` ("veil") at 11:10. No early Greek manuscript has this reading; it appears in Latin and Bohairic translations and in patristic citations.
2. `κάλυμμα` denotes a garment whose purpose was head **and** face (Nõmmik 2025: 301). Once the verse was read as "a woman ought to have a *veil* on her head," the question of the veil's extent became live.
3. Ascetic anxiety about women's visibility, plus reading the passage through 1 Tim 2 and Eph 5, moved the focus to the face.

> "Beginning with the end of the second century CE, many interpreters of this passage focused their attention solely on the necessity for women to cover their heads and faces." — Nõmmik 2025: 301

The variant is therefore not just a textual curiosity; it is the hinge of the whole later reading.

---

## 3. Clement of Alexandria (c. 150–215; *Paedagogus* c. 190–200)

**The earliest Christian text that clearly requires the face.**

> "it has also been enjoined that the head should be veiled and the face covered" — *Paed.* 2.10.114, as quoted in Leone 2012: 281 n.22

> women should wear a covering "that conceals their eyes" … and "have been commanded … to cover their heads and to conceal their faces" — *Paed.* 2.10.106–107, paraphrased in Nõmmik 2025: 301

> "by not bearing her face, but covering it with a veil, she also prevents anyone else from sinning" — *Paed.* 3.11.79, in Nõmmik 2025: 302

**Three qualifications that matter:**

1. Clement **paraphrases** 1 Cor 11:13 rather than quoting it.
2. He uses `ἐγκαλύπτω` (complete concealment, often associated with shame) rather than Paul's `κατακαλύπτω` (Nõmmik 2025: 302).
3. He grounds the practice in female "weakness" (`διὰ τὴν ἀσθένειαν`) — a rationale Paul never gives.

So Clement is evidence for *Christian face-covering*, not for *Pauline face-covering*. He is the first datable Christian requirement, and it is an ascetic extension, not an exegesis.

---

## 4. Tertullian (c. 155–220; *De virginibus velandis* c. 200–213)

Tertullian is the most-cited and most-misused witness. What he actually says:

> "Arabia's heathen females will be your judges, who cover not only the head, but the face also, so entirely, that they are content, with one eye free, to enjoy rather than half the light than to prostitute the entire face." — *Virg.* 17

This is an **admiring comparison with pagan Arabs**, not a description of Christian practice — and not a command. The Christians he is attacking are going about with "front quite bare" (or with token coverings that do not reach the ears).

His own definition of the veil's extent is head-and-neck, measured by the hairline:

> "Let them know that the whole head constitutes the woman. Its limits and boundaries reach as far as the place where the robe begins. The region of the veil is co-extensive with the space covered by the hair when unbound; in order that the necks too may be encircled." — *Virg.* 17

And elsewhere:

> "who will have the audacity to intrude with his eyes upon a shrouded face?" — *Virg.* 15

**Assessment:** Tertullian *admires* face-covering and uses "shrouded face" rhetoric, but his stated rule is head, ears and neck. Radle's judgement is that he "does not advocate facial veils for Christian women" (2019: 1085 n.71). Upson-Saia and Leone read the "shrouded face" language as implying more (Upson-Saia 2011: 62–63; Leone 2012: 282–283). The disagreement is real; the weight of his own definition favours head/neck.

**Context matters:** the treatise is a polemic against Carthaginian virgins who refused the veil in church. It is evidence of *contested practice*, not of settled custom.

---

## 5. Didascalia Apostolorum (N. Syria, c. 230)

> "when thou walkest in the street, cover thy head with thy robe, that by reason of thy veil thy great beauty may be hidden. And adorn not thy natural face; but walk with downcast looks, being veiled." — *Didasc.* 1.8–9 (Gibson translation)

> "if thou coverest thy face from strange men with a veil of modesty, how then canst thou go in with strange men to a bath?" — *Didasc.* 1.8–9

**Assessment:** the head is covered with the robe; the face is presumed to be concealed in public. This is the clearest early *church-order* evidence for face-covering outside Alexandria. It is Syrian, third-century, and ascetic in tone.

---

## 6. Cyprian (c. 200–258, Carthage)

*De habitu virginum* deals extensively with hair, dress, cosmetics and baths — and does **not** mention a face-veil (Nõmmik 2025: 309). A generation after Tertullian's campaign in the same city, the face is not part of the discussion.

**Assessment:** this is a useful negative control. If face-covering were the settled Carthaginian norm, Cyprian's silence would be odd.

---

## 7. Chrysostom (c. 347–407, Antioch/Constantinople)

> "men and women are marked by different characteristics — the man to rule, the woman to be subject. She covers her head while he keeps his bare." — *Hom. 1 Cor.* 26, in Kovacs 2005: 179

Chrysostom treats the covering as dress, like a "mantle or a veil," and reads it as the sign of subjection. He speaks of the head being wrapped "on all sides" (Radle 2019: 1086) — head and hair, not face.

**Assessment:** the greatest Greek commentator on Paul says nothing about the face. This is significant because he is arguing precisely about what the covering is and what it signifies.

---

## 8. Theodoret (c. 393–457)

> "Paul calls the veil authority. This means: let her show her subjection, making herself humble, not least on account of the angels…" — on 11:10, in Kovacs 2005: 183

Again: veil = authority, no face.

---

## 9. Jerome and Ambrose (late 4th c.)

- **Jerome**, *Ep.* 130.18 (414): praises a woman who "veils all of her face except her eyes" (Radle 2019: 1086 n.77).
- **Ambrose**, *De poenitentia* 1.14: covers "head (`caput`)" and "face (`vultus`)" (Radle 2019: 1086 n.77).

**Assessment:** both are describing an ascetic ideal — the consecrated virgin or widow — not a universal rule for all Christian women. They show that face-covering was admired and practised in elite ascetic circles in the West.

---

## 10. Byzantium

Radle 2019 is the fullest treatment in the corpus.

- The Byzantine veil was primarily about **hair and head**, and functioned as a rite-of-passage marker for girls and women.
- "References to facial coverage are quite rare" (Radle 2019: 1092).
- The fullest case — Psellos' mother in the eleventh century — "veiled her entire face with modesty," yet remained recognisable (Radle 2019: 1091–1092).
- By the later period, girls before puberty covered in church, "an expectation not found in any of the patristic sources" (Radle 2019: 1093).

**Assessment:** Byzantium is a head-and-hair culture, with occasional elite face-veiling.

---

## 11. Later Europe

- **16th-century Venice:** Vecellio describes girls wearing a `fazzuolo` "big enough to cover face and bosom," and adult women a `cappa` that "shielded their face … but also allowed them to see out" (Burghartz 2015: 4–5).
- **Spain:** the `tapada` — face covered except one eye — read as "seductive, mysterious and rebellious," not simply modest (Burghartz 2015: 6).
- In both cases the face-covering is **fashion and moral anxiety**, not a reading of 1 Cor 11.

## 13. Verdict

| Proposition | Verdict | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Face-covering is attested in early Christianity | True (from c. 190) | high |
| Face-covering was required by the earliest Christian writers | False — the earliest (Chrysostom, Theodoret) say head only | high |
| Face-covering was universal in the early church | False | high |
| Face-covering was an ascetic and regional extension | True | high |
| The face-reading was driven partly by the `κάλυμμα` variant | Well-supported | high |
| Face-covering is a later Islamic import | False | high |

**The pattern:** head covering everywhere; face covering as a regional and ascetic intensification, first clearly required in Alexandria around 190–200, admired in Carthage and Syria, practised by elite ascetics in the West, rare in Byzantium, and never made universal.
