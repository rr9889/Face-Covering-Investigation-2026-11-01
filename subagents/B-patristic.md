## Face-covering evidence log (1 Cor 11:2–16)

Harmon's article is absent; nothing below is attributed to him. Also note: the .pdf files in `\Head Coverings\` are real PDFs, not text; use the `hc_extract` .txt twins.

### Upson-Saia, *Early Christian Dress* (2011), ch. 3 (both files)

- [EVIDENCE] Tertullian, *De virginibus velandis* 17: "pagan women of Arabia" wear head AND face covering, "content with one eye free to enjoy half the light, rather than to prostitute the whole face" (pp. 62–63) — earliest Christian mention of face-covering, framed as foreign excess.
- [EVIDENCE] Carthaginian women covered "only partially with 'turbans and wool' hats or 'strips of linen'"; some went "with their foreheads distinctly uncovered" (p. 62) — the live norm was head-only and contested.

### Leone, *Cultures of Invisibility* (2012)

- [EVIDENCE] Clement of Alexandria, *Paedagogus* II.x.114.3–4: "it has also been enjoined that the head should be veiled and the face covered" (p. 281 n. 22) — the clearest Christian text requiring face-covering; Alexandria, c. 190–200.
- [EVIDENCE] Tertullian, *Virg.* 15.4: "who will have the audacity to intrude with his eyes upon a shrouded face?" (p. 282). But *Virg.* 17.3: "The region of the veil is co-extensive with the space covered by the hair when unbound" (p. 283).
- [INTERPRETATION] The veil does "not so much protect the female body from male desire, as defend male desire from the female body" (p. 281).

### Radle, "Veiling of Women in Byzantium" (2019)

- [INTERPRETATION] Tertullian "does not advocate facial veils for Christian women," though he admires Arabian practice (p. 1085 n. 71).
- [EVIDENCE] *Didascalia apostolorum* 1.8–9 (c. 230, N. Syria): "cover your head with your robe so that your great beauty is concealed," and it assumes "you conceal your face" in public (p. 1086).
- [EVIDENCE] Jerome, *Ep.* 130.18 (414): praises a woman who "veils all of her face except her eyes"; Ambrose, *De poenitentia* 1.14, covers "head (caput)" and "face (vultus)" (p. 1086 n. 77).
- [EVIDENCE] Chrysostom: covered "with all care and diligence," "wrapping it up on all sides" (p. 1086) — head and hair, not face.
- [INTERPRETATION] "References to facial coverage are quite rare" (p. 1092); the fullest case, Psellos' mother (11th c.), "veiled her entire face with modesty," yet remained recognisable. By then girls before puberty covered in church, "an expectation not found in any of the patristic sources" (p. 1093).

### Burghartz, "Covered Women" (2015)

- [EVIDENCE] Vecellio (1590s): Venetian girls wore "a veil of white silk known as a 'fazzuolo', big enough to cover face and bosom"; adult cappa "shielded their face… but also allowed them to see out" (pp. 4–5).
- [INTERPRETATION] The Spanish tapada was read as "seductive, mysterious and rebellious," not modest (p. 6).

### Galter, "Veil and Headscarf" (2021) — pre-Christian background

- [EVIDENCE] Mesopotamian hem (sissiktu) "could… also be used to cover the head and sometimes the face" (p. 22); Isaiah's re'ala is "apparently a small face veil" (p. 24).
- [INTERPRETATION] "From the 2nd century CE onward, veiling seemed to be common practice in Palestine and the Middle East" (p. 24).

### *Covered Glory* (popular tract; the head-only case)

- [INTERPRETATION] The Greek terms "indicate a garment that covers over the top of the head rather than covering the front of the face"; the passage gives "no indication that Paul was concerned about covered or uncovered faces" (p. 5).
- [EVIDENCE] Uses Tertullian's contrast: pagan women "cover not only the head, but the face also" (p. 5); cites Shepherd of Hermas *Vis.* 4.2.1 for a head-covering worn with a separate face-veil. BDAG confirms Hermas: "veiled to the forehead" (heos tou metopou) — a covering stopping at the brow.

### Kovacs, *1 Corinthians Interpreted…* (2005)

- [EVIDENCE] Chrysostom, *Hom.* 26: "She covers her head while he keeps his bare" (p. 179); the covering is treated as dress, like a "mantle or a veil."
- [EVIDENCE] Theodoret on 11:10: "Paul calls the veil authority" (p. 183). Kovacs n. 8: "RSV: 'a veil.' Others translate 'a sign of authority.'"
- [INTERPRETATION] "Paul's main concern is to tell women to cover their heads when they prophesy" (p. 177).

### Bray & Oden, *Ancient Christian Commentary* (2006)

- [EVIDENCE] Ambrosiaster: "The veil signifies power, and the angels are bishops" (p. 105); Chrysostom 26.5: "Being covered is a mark of subjection and authority." At 11:10: "Greek authority (the veil being a symbol of this)" (p. 103).
- [INTERPRETATION] NO excerpt in either catena mentions a face veil. The reception is entirely about head, hair, and authority.

### Supporting checks

- BDAG s.v. kalumma (Ps.-Dicaearchus, Theban women): the head-covering is "of such a kind that the whole face seems to be covered as with a mask" — the word CAN mean full-face coverage, but need not.
- MacMullen, in Smith (2014, p. 19 n. 52): "Veiling meant the head, only in Arabia the face as well."
- **VARIANT AT 11:10:** NA reads exousian; no early Greek MS reads kalumma, yet Irenaeus (*Haer.* 1.8.2) and "Hippolytus" (*Comm. Dan.*) both quote kalumma, as do later Latin and Bohairic witnesses. Nõmmik: it "does not appear to be a scribal 'corruption'… but seems to have circulated at first independently" (pp. 300–301); with kalumma, "beginning with the end of the second century CE, many interpreters… focused… solely on the necessity for women to cover their heads and faces" (p. 301). Reading and variant are entangled.

## Timeline of face-covering in Christian sources

- **8th–5th c. BCE, Judah/Greece** — re'ala, a small face veil (Isa 3:18–23; Galter p. 24); tegidion with eye-holes, Theban women fully covered (Montier pp. 24–25).
- **1st c. CE, Corinth** — Paul argues from kephale and katakalupto; prosopon never appears.
- **c. 190–200, Alexandria** — Clement: head veiled AND face covered (*Paed.* 2.10.114; 3.11.79).
- **c. 200–213, Carthage** — Tertullian: head, ears, neck; Arabian one-eye veiling admired, not imposed (*Virg.* 17).
- **c. 230, N. Syria** — Didascalia: robe over the head; face-concealment presumed (1.8–9).
- **c. 250, Carthage** — Cyprian, *De habitu virginum*: hair, dress, baths, cosmetics — NO veils mentioned (Nõmmik p. 309).
- **365 Gangra / 390 Cod. Theod.** — legislation on hair, not faces.
- **c. 390–414, Antioch and Rome** — Chrysostom: head wrapped "on all sides," no face; Jerome: full face veil except the eyes, praised in one ascetic; Ambrose (Milan): caput and vultus.
- **11th–12th c., Constantinople** — Psellos' mother veils "her entire face"; Irene Doukaina uses a face veil as disguise (Radle pp. 1091–92).
- **1523–1590s, Spain/Italy** — tapada, fazzuolo, cappa become fashion and are moralised against.

## Where sources disagree

1. **Does Tertullian require the face?** Radle: no (p. 1085 n. 71). Leone and Upson-Saia: his "shrouded face" language implies it. Since *Virg.* 17.3 defines the veil by hair-line and neck, the weight is head/neck; the face language is rhetorical.
2. **Christian and early, or foreign and optional?** Clement requires it; Tertullian attributes it to pagan Arabs; *Covered Glory* and MacMullen place it in Arabia, not the Greco-Roman world.
3. **Does Paul's vocabulary mean a face veil?** *Covered Glory*: no. Nõmmik: kalumma could cover head and face, and the variant pushed readers toward the face.
4. **When does a Christian face norm begin?** Nõmmik: "end of the second century" (p. 301). Radle: facial coverage "quite rare," never a general norm (p. 1092).

## What remains unknown

- No Christian canonical or liturgical text BEFORE Clement commands the face; whether his ruling was enforced or was ascetic advice is unknown.
- Whether Jerome and Ambrose describe a widespread custom or an idealised elite ascetic.
- No systematic survey here of face-covering in Christian art; catacomb and Dura-Europos images show veiled heads with faces visible (Radle p. 1094).
- Direction of causation at 11:10; regional practice (Egypt, Syria, Asia Minor, Gaul) is otherwise unattested.

## Bottom line (as returned)

Paul and the whole Greek patristic reception through Chrysostom speak of the HEAD (kephale), never the face (prosopon); the first Christian text to require the face is Clement of Alexandria c. 190–200, and face-covering thereafter appears as (a) an admired foreign/Arabian custom in Tertullian, (b) an ascetic ideal in Jerome and Ambrose, (c) rare in Byzantium, and (d) a European fashion from the 16th c. The kalumma variant at 11:10 is a probable driver of the face-reading.
