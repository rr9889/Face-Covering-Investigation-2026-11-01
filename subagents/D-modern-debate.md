## 1 Cor 11:2–16 and the "face-veil" question — neutral source review

**Headline finding:** no ancient source describes Corinthian Christian women wearing face-veils. The face-veil reading entered the tradition through three independent doors: a text-critical accident (ἐξουσία → κάλυμμα at 11:10 in patristic citation), a 17th–19th-century reconstruction of a Jewish face-covering custom from rabbinic sources, and modern functional/analogical readings (pollution, visibility, Islamic comparison). No modern peer-reviewed scholar in this corpus defends a literal Corinthian face-veil on Corinthian evidence.

### The four questions

**(a) No.** Nothing describes Corinthian women in face-veils. The nearest data are elsewhere: Tarsus (Dio Chrys.), Arabia (Tertullian), rabbinic Judea (Jeremias), Roman brides (*flammeum*, over head not face). Positive counter-evidence: Corinthian portraits bare-headed (Gill p. 251).

**(b) Yes — but the defenders are patristic, early-modern, or analogical:** Clement (*Paed.* 2.10.107; 3.11.79), Tertullian's admiration for Arabian practice (*Virg.* 17.2), Lightfoot (17th c.), von Mosheim and Michaelis (18th c.), Robertson & Plummer 1914 (reporting "removal of facial coverings," Nõmmik p. 39), Jeremias 1969 as the scholarly warrant. Grounds: (i) inferred Jewish custom; (ii) the semantic range of κάλυμμα/κατακαλύπτω; (iii) the functional argument that a head-only covering cannot stop the gaze.

**(c)** Nõmmik: the reading arose c. 190–220 CE, not with Paul. First, some readers replaced ἐξουσία with κάλυμμα at 11:10 (no Greek MS support), making "veil" the subject of the verse. Second, because κάλυμμα denotes an item whose purpose was head *and* face, focus shifted to faces from the end of the second century. Third, ascetic anxiety about women's visibility plus reading through 1 Tim 2/Eph 5 completed the shift (pp. 300–301, 315–317).

**(d) No corpus of Christian women with covered faces.** Catacomb figures wear head-only caps (Marlowe p. 5); Dura Europos synagogue women are head-covered, faces visible (Marlowe p. 9); the single face-wrapped statuette is anomalous (p. 3); Byzantine face-veils appear only in later medieval rites (Radle, p. 1218 region). The claim of early Christian face-veiling is asserted, not shown.

### Three positions

1. **Head-covering only, face visible** (Marlowe, Gill, Nõmmik, Fee via Wu; Payne by implication).
2. **Functional total concealment including face** (Clement; Tertullian on virgins; modern Martin, Leone) — argued from purpose and anxiety, not from Corinthian data.
3. **Not a cloth at all** — hair/hairstyle (Payne; the older "long hair" tradition).

Plus a fourth, increasingly common: **the referent is indeterminate** (Marshall; Wu's definitional breadth).

### Weakest links on each side

*Pro-face-veil:* zero Corinthian evidence; Jeremias's custom rests on late rabbinic material (Nõmmik p. 35 n. 13); Clement paraphrases rather than quotes 11:13 and imports "weakness"; Tertullian himself defines the veil's extent as co-extensive with the hair, not the face; the hetaera argument has a documented no-source genealogy.

*Anti-face-veil:* the argument from silence is weaker than it looks — Paul names no garment at all except περιβόλαιον, so the absence of κάλυμμα proves little; and if the aim is blocking the gaze, a head-only covering is functionally thin. Both sides over-read the κατα- prefix as "thoroughly covered".

### Unresolved

What the Corinthian women actually wore (palla? ricinium? hair only?); the identity of the angels (if Watchers, the "cover the face too" logic gains force); whether 11:10's ἐξουσία is itself a veil; the absence of any systematic art-historical corpus coded for face visibility, with catacomb evidence late; and the origin of the κάλυμμα reading (Rome? Hippolytus?) — which Nõmmik himself leaves open (p. 302 n. 22).
