# 10 — Open questions

> **[Audit 2026-11-07]** A revised, prioritised list of next steps, including the Josephus *Ant.* 7.254 check and a full concordance of `κατακαλύπτω`, is in `12-revised-method-and-assessment.md` §7–8.

This file records what the investigation could **not** settle, where the verdicts are weakest, and what evidence would change them. It is deliberately written as a challenge to the report rather than a defence of it.

---

## 1. What remains genuinely unknown

### 1.1 What the Corinthian women actually wore

We do not know whether the covering was a `palla`, a `ricinium`, a `himation`, a headscarf, a snood, or — on the hair-thesis reading — simply hair. Without that, "did it cover the face?" has no definite answer at the level of *practice*, even if it has one at the level of *text*.

**What would settle it:** a Corinthian find or image of a Christian woman; a first-century Corinthian inventory or will mentioning women's garments; a fuller reading of the Andania/Lycosura inscriptions.

### 1.2 Whether the Jews of Paul's time covered the face

The Jeremias reconstruction (two veils, head-band, chin-bands) would make face-covering a plausible Jewish background. But the sources are late, and the best commentator on the Jewish material (Barrett) denies the face.

**What would settle it:** a first-century Jewish text or image specifying face-covering; a re-examination of the rabbinic parallels with dating.

### 1.3 Whether Greek women normally covered the face in public

Llewellyn-Jones says the veil routinely covered head and sometimes face; Smith and Marlowe say the ordinary covering left the face visible. The disagreement is partly definitional, partly evidential.

**What would settle it:** a systematic corpus of Greek vase painting and sculpture coded for face visibility; a lexical study of `καλύπτω` + body-part in classical Greek.

### 1.4 The identity of the angels

If they are the Watchers, the "cover the face too" logic gains force. If they are guardian angels, it does not. If they are the seraphim of Isa 6:2, the face reading gets its only internal foothold.

**What would settle it:** a study of the angels tradition in Paul's churches; the variant readings and their theological contexts.

### 1.5 The origin of the `κάλυμμα` reading at 11:10

Nõmmik leaves it open (p. 302 n.22): Rome? Hippolytus? A Latin translational tradition? The direction of causation — did the variant create the practice, or the practice create the variant? — is unresolved.

**What would settle it:** a full apparatus of the patristic citations; a study of the Old Latin and Bohairic transmission.

### 1.6 What Clement's ruling actually did

Was Clement's requirement enforced in Alexandria? Was it advice to ascetics? We have no evidence of reception.

**What would settle it:** later Alexandrian catechetical and canonical material; documentary evidence (letters, sermons) from Egypt.

## 2. What would overturn each verdict

| Verdict | Would be overturned by |
|---|---|
| **Paul does not require the face** | A first-century text using `κατακαλύπτω` with a woman's face as object; a demonstration that `κάλυμμα` at 11:10 is original; a pre-Pauline Christian tradition requiring the face |
| **Paul does not forbid the face** | A patristic or NT text treating face-covering as sinful; a canonical prohibition |
| **The covering normally left the face open** | A systematic art corpus showing face-covering as the norm; a Roman legal or literary text making face-covering obligatory |
| **Face covering is early but regional** | Evidence of face-covering in the first century, or in the West before Clement |
| **The face reading arose c. 190–220** | A first-century or early-second-century Christian text requiring the face |
| **Face covering is not an Islamic import** | Evidence that all Christian face-covering derives from post-7th-century contact — which the Clement and Didascalia texts rule out |

## 3. Suggested next steps

Listed in order of likely payoff:

1. **Read Oster 1988 in full.** It is the one major article in the corpus that is present only as an abstract. His treatment of male veiling is the background for 11:4; his view of women's veils is unknown.
2. **Collate Clement's *Paedagogus* directly.** The key text (2.10; 3.11) is quoted only second-hand here. The Greek should be checked, especially the verbs (`ἐγκαλύπτω` vs `κατακαλύπτω`) and the rationale (`διὰ τὴν ἀσθένειαν`).
3. **Check the NA28/ECM apparatus at 11:10** for the exact witness list of the `κάλυμμα` reading. This would firm up or correct Nõmmik's account.
4. **Consult Llewellyn-Jones, *Aphrodite's Tortoise*** directly, alongside Smith's critique. The Greek-veil question hinges on this disagreement.
5. **Examine the catacomb and Dura-Europos material** in a proper art-historical corpus, coded for face visibility. The current dossier relies on second-hand reports.
6. **Check the Syriac and Coptic versions** of 1 Cor 11:10 and 11:5. If the Peshitta reads "veil" rather than "authority," that changes the Eastern reception picture.
7. **Read the *Didascalia* in Syriac** (Gibson's translation was used here, which is serviceable but not critical).
8. **Look at Byzantine liturgical texts** for the veil's role, beyond Radle's article.

## 4. Questions not asked

These are adjacent and were deliberately excluded; they matter for the wider debate but not for the face question:

- **Hair or cloth?** If Paul means hair, the face question is moot. This dossier assumes the cloth reading (BDAG, Fee, Massey, Thiselton) but records the minority (Payne, and the older Isaksson line).
- **Headship semantics.** Whether `κεφαλή` means "source," "authority," or "pre-eminence" does not affect the face question, but it dominates the literature.
- **Men's veiling.** The male half of the passage (11:4) deserves its own study; it was used here only as a control on what a "head covering" looked like.
- **The "authority" (`ἐξουσία`) question.** Whether the woman's `ἐξουσία` is her own authority, the man's, or the veil itself is unresolved and is treated here only where it touches the variant.
- **Contemporary application.** The normative question was answered in the narrow sense ("ought it to be included?"), not in the broader sense of what a church today should require.

## 5. Where this report is weakest

Being explicit about the soft spots:

1. **The "probable" verdict on Claim 4.** The culture's default is clear, but the Oxford/Keener/Héring/Goulder counter-case is mainstream, not fringe. If they are right, Claim 4 becomes "contested."
2. **The dependence on Nõmmik for the reception history.** The reconstruction is elegant and well-documented, but it is one scholar's synthesis, and the causal direction (variant → practice, or practice → variant) is not established.
3. **The art argument.** It rests on Stafford's corpus and Gill's portraits, neither of which was designed to answer this question. A dedicated corpus could shift it.
4. **The Clement dating.** c. 190–200 is conventional but the *Paedagogus* is not dated precisely, and the relevant chapters may be earlier or later.
5. **The Jewish background.** Jeremias is old scholarship and the rabbinic sources are late; Barrett's scepticism may be right.
6. **The patristic selection.** The corpus contains many Fathers only in catena form (Kovacs, Bray & Oden). A fuller patristic search (TLG, the *Corpus Christianorum*) would be more reliable than the excerpts available here.

## 6. A standing test for future claims

Any future claim in this debate should be able to answer three questions:

1. **Is this evidence, or interpretation?** (A lexicon entry is evidence; "the veil must have covered the face because modesty" is interpretation.)
2. **What is the date and provenance of the source?** (Clement is Alexandria, c. 190; the *Didascalia* is Syria, c. 230; Jerome is Bethlehem, 414. None of them is Corinth, 50.)
3. **Does it describe practice, prescribe practice, or merely admire practice?** (Tertullian *admires* the Arab face-veil and *prescribes* the head-veil. Clement *prescribes* both. Paul *prescribes* the head.)

Most of the confusion in the secondary literature comes from failing the third test.
