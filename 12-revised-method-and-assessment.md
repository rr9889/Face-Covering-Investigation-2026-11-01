# 12 — Revised method and assessment (7 November 2026)

This replaces the method in `01` and the verdicts in `07` as the dossier's working conclusions. `01`–`10` are kept as the record of the first pass, with audit notes where they were wrong (see `11-independent-audit.md`).

---

## 1. The question, stated so it can be answered

The original question, "did the head covering include the face?", hides three different questions. Each needs different evidence, and an answer to one is not an answer to another.

| | Question | Kind of evidence | Can it be settled? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Q1 — Text** | Does the wording of 1 Cor 11:2–16 require, forbid, or leave open covering the face? | Greek text, grammar, Paul's usage | Yes, largely |
| **Q2 — History** | What did the covering Paul presupposed **usually look like** for women praying or prophesying in Corinth c. 53–55 CE? | Dress practice in Corinth and Paul's world; male parallel; earliest Christian descriptions | Only probabilistically |
| **Q3 — Reception** | When, where and why did Christians read the passage as including the face? | Patristic texts, church orders, textual variants, art | Partly |

A normative question ("ought it to be included today?") depends on theological premises about how Q1–Q3 bind a reader. It is **out of scope** for a historical dossier, and the revised assessment does not answer it. (The original `07` did.)

## 2. Competing hypotheses for Q2, with predictions written before weighing

| Hypothesis | Claim | What we should expect to find if it is true |
|---|---|---|
| **H-hair** | The "covering" is hair or hairstyle; no garment is meant | Paul's covering words used of hair; no garment vocabulary; patristic readers puzzled |
| **H-open** | A garment drawn over the head and hair, face normally visible | Roman/Corinthian women and men shown or described with head draped, face clear; earliest Christian descriptions with the face visible; Paul's argument about hair, not gaze |
| **H-face** | The normal form was drawn over the face (fully or partly) | Corinthian or Pauline-world sources describing face veils as ordinary; earliest Christian descriptions with the face hidden; Paul's argument mentioning sight, eyes or face |
| **H-variable** | Paul's rule fixes only "head/hair covered"; how far the garment came down varied by place and person and was outside his concern | Mixed regional evidence; Paul's criterion stated purely in head/hair terms; early Christian practice divided by region |

These are not mutually exclusive in every respect: **H-variable** overlaps the others. It is listed separately because a mixed body of evidence is itself a finding, not a failure to decide.

## 3. Rules of evidence (what changed from the first pass)

1. **Silence is coded as silence.** A source that does not mention the face is "silent", never "head only". Only an explicit statement counts for or against the face. (Fixes audit A4.)
2. **Concordance before example.** A lexical claim is tested against every occurrence in the relevant corpus (LXX, NT, Apostolic Fathers), not against one or two chosen cases. (Fixes A2, A3.)
3. **Weigh by proximity.** Each item is graded on place (Corinth > Roman Greece > Greek East > elsewhere), date (1st c. CE > Hellenistic > Classical; for reception, earlier > later) and genre (description of practice > prescription > allegory or polemic).
4. **Name each evidence class's built-in bias.** Portraits over-represent faces; ascetic texts over-represent strict practice; polemical texts describe what the author opposes.
5. **Symmetric search.** For each hypothesis, run the searches most likely to *disconfirm* it, and record what they return. (The first pass's face-context scan was symmetric in design, but its reading list and the Greek-East discussion were not; see A7.)
6. **No numbers without a model.** Confidence is stated in words, with the reason.
7. **First attestation ≠ origin.** In a period with few sources, "not attested before X" is weak evidence that something did not exist before X.
8. **Primary text over report.** Every load-bearing quotation is checked against the primary text or marked "second-hand".

## 5. Revised assessment

### Q1 — What the text requires

- **The text requires the head (hair) to be covered.** Confidence: **high.** (H-hair remains a serious minority reading; most lexica and commentaries reject it.)
- **The text neither requires nor forbids covering the face.** Confidence: **high.** The face is not mentioned; nothing in the argument depends on it either way.
- **The rule's criterion is the head, not the face.** Confidence: **high.** (E1–E3.) This is the true content of the original's "strongest argument", without its false lexical premise (audit A1).

### Q2 — What the covering usually looked like at Corinth

- **Leaning:** H-open, a head-drape with the face normally visible, is **more likely than not** for Corinth itself. The reasons are the Roman-colony setting (E7), the probable male parallel (E8), the face-centred Roman and Western Christian evidence (E5, E13), and Paul's head-and-hair logic (E1–E3).
- **But the margin is smaller than the first pass claimed.** Face-veiling was a respectable practice in parts of the Greek world (E6, E10), including Paul's home city (E11). The verb carries no head-only bias (E4). The portrait evidence is weak (E9). Some women in a mixed eastern congregation may well have drawn the mantle over part of the face, and nothing in Paul's wording would make that non-compliant.
- **Overall confidence:** **probable, not high.** H-variable describes the evidence well: **one rule (head/hair covered), more than one way of keeping it.**

### Q3 — Reception

- **The first explicit Christian evidence for covering the face is Clement of Alexandria, c. 195.** Confidence: **high** (verified).
- **Whether the practice began then cannot be said.** The 2nd-century record is too thin (audit A10). Confidence that it *arose* c. 190–220: **low**.
- **The earliest Christian evidence is regionally split:** face visible in Rome (Hermas) and Carthage (Tertullian); face covered in Alexandria (Clement) and later Syria (Didascalia). Confidence: **high** for the split, **probable** for reading it as an East/West pattern (few data points).
- **Face-covering was never universal Christian practice, and it is not an Islamic import.** Confidence: **high** (unchanged).

## 7. What would change this assessment

- **Toward H-face:** a 1st-c. CE text or image from Corinth or Roman Greece describing respectable women's ordinary dress as covering the face; a coded figurine corpus showing lower-face veiling as common in Roman-period Greece. (Josephus *Ant.* 7.254 has now been checked: it shows the absolute middle is not head-restricted, but it concerns a mourning gesture, so it does not move Q2.)
- **Toward H-open:** a coded corpus of non-portrait images (figurines, reliefs, wall-paintings) from Roman Corinth showing draped heads with faces clear; a 2nd-c. Christian text from Greece or Asia Minor describing worship dress with the face visible.
- **Toward H-hair:** Paul's covering terms shown to be used of hair alone in comparable Greek.
